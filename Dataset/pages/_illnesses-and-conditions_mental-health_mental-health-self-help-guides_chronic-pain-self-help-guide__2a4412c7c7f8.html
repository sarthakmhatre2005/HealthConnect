Chronic pain and your mental health

1. Introduction

Urgent help

This self-help guide is intended for people with mild-to-moderate mental health issues.

If you're feeling distressed, in a state of despair, suicidal or in need of emotional support you can phone NHS 24 on 111.

For an emergency ambulance phone 999.

Chronic pain affects over a third of adults in the UK. It can have a huge impact on your quality of life, and can also affect the lives of your family and those around you.

This guide offers a number of techniques for helping you manage the effect of chronic pain on your mental health.

How to use this guide

Working through this guide can take around 30 to 40 minutes, but you should feel free to work at your own pace.

Work through the guide on your device, using the “Next” button to move forward and use the “Previous” button instead of the Back button in your browser.

Section 2 of 20

2. Understanding chronic pain

Chronic pain can be caused by an injury or health condition. It’s called chronic pain once it’s lasted for longer than 3 months after the usual recovery time. For example, if you injure your back and it’s still painful after 3 months, or if you have a condition like endometriosis that causes pain on a regular basis.

Pain can start with a definite problem at a specific time, or it can come on gradually for no obvious reason. It can also come on some time after an event, even if you felt fine at the time.

You might feel the pain in a specific part of your body, or it could feel like it’s everywhere.

Even if you do things like take medication or rest, chronic pain might not go away. The pain can be there all the time, or it can come and go. Sometimes you might feel more sensitive to pain, and sometimes it can flare up – meaning it can become very bad – or get worse quickly and unexpectedly. At other times you might feel fine, or have minimal pain.

Accepting that chronic pain is part of your life can be very helpful in improving your mental health. This video from Pain Association Scotland has more information.

Get more advice from Live Well With Pain

Home and work life

  • You might find you have less energy to do the things you enjoy.
  • It can be stressful to manage work, home, and other commitments when dealing with pain.
  • It might be harder to concentrate on work or study when dealing with pain.
  • You might struggle to get enough sleep, leaving you tired and struggling to cope.

Anxiety and fear

  • You could feel anxious or scared about what’s causing the pain, and worried about damaging your body.
  • Many people feel worried about the future, and about living with pain for a long time.

Other challenging emotions

  • You could find yourself being irritable with the people you care about due to the pain.
  • You might feel hopeless or down about the pain, which can lead to depression.
  • Some people feel angry – either with people they meet who don’t understand the pain, or with the pain itself.
  • Others feel angry with themselves – they see pain as a sign of weakness, and resting up as “lazy” or “letting the pain win”.

3. How pain affects your feelings and thoughts

Living with chronic pain can affect a number of areas of your life, in a lot of different ways.

Home and work life

  • you might have less energy to do the things you enjoy
  • it can be stressful to manage work, home, and other commitments when dealing with pain
  • it might be harder to concentrate on work or study when dealing with pain
  • you might struggle to get enough sleep

Anxiety and fear

  • you might feel anxious or scared about what’s causing the pain
  • you might worry about damaging your body
  • you may be concerned about doing things to make the pain worse
  • you might worry about the future
  • you might feel anxious at the thought of living with pain for a long time

Other challenging emotions

  • you could find yourself being irritable with the people you care about due to the pain
  • you might feel hopeless or down because of the pain
  • some people feel angry – either with people who don’t understand the pain, or the pain itself
  • some people feel angry with themselves – they see pain as a sign of weakness, and resting up as “lazy” or “letting the pain win”

The way people think and feel about pain affects how they experience the pain, and also how they cope with it. If you feel pain every time you perform a task or activity, it’s unlikely you’ll want to continue with it, or do that activity again. This can make you feel down, or create feelings of guilt or anger.

When pain affects your ability to do the things you want and need to do, it can help your mental health to find ways of working around it. It also helps to rest when you need to.

Section 4 of 20

4. Coping with pain

People cope with chronic pain in different ways. Often you’ll find that some things you do are helpful, while others can be less helpful.

Pushing through pain

Some people try to “push through” the pain, and refuse to “let the pain win.” This means that they often end up feeling worse, and able to do less than they would have if they’d spent some time resting.

Anne refused to stop and rest – she had work to do and children to look after, and her motto was “no pain, no gain”.

She often felt low, upset, and frustrated with herself and the pain. By the time Anne went back to her doctor to seek more help, she was managing to do very little, and felt like giving up.

Avoiding pain

Patterns often develop as people try to manage their pain and perform day-to-day activities. Some people feel trapped or controlled by their pain, so they use a lot of energy trying to avoid it.

Sandra had been dealing with chronic pain for a while, and felt like she couldn’t keep trying to “beat the pain”. She started avoiding doing too much in case the pain flared up – she felt she could manage the pain, even if she wasn’t very active. By the time she discussed the pain with her doctor again, she had a poor quality of life, often felt low and sad, and felt overall more tired and less able to do things she enjoyed.

Up and down

Some people do more on good days, and only rest on bad days. Sometimes this means you get a lot done on good days, but end up having a bad day afterwards. This makes it hard to plan anything, and can leave you feeling as if you aren’t in control of your life. Your activity levels go up and down all the time.

Jim wanted to get things done when he had less pain, because activities were easier and he had more energy. Sometimes he did quite a lot on a good day. However, he usually had a bad day the next day, or a few days later. On bad days, he’d rest and recover until the pain got better, and then he’d start doing a lot again.

Jim found it hard to make plans, and stopped doing any activity immediately if he felt the pain starting. He went back to his doctor and said he felt like he didn’t get to decide what he did – it was the pain making the choice for him.

Later on in the guide we’ll look at ways for you to take control of how you cope with your pain, improving your mental health.

5. Taking control of your pain

Some people feel pain all or most of the time, and it’s always in the background. It can be difficult for them to cope when it flares up.

It’s important to learn the things that make your pain worse – often called pain “triggers” – so you can find ways to manage them. It’s better to take a balanced approach to being active, so you don’t do too much or too little.

This can include:

  • finding ways to pace activities, so you can build up your activity level
  • finding ways to space activities, so you can break them down into smaller parts and take more breaks
  • finding solutions to work around your pain

The next three sections cover pacing, spacing, and finding solutions. If any of these sections don’t apply to you, feel free to skip to the next one.

Section 6 of 20

6. Pacing activities

Whether or not you can pace activities depends on the type and cause of your chronic pain. If you’re unsure, talk to your doctor.

The aim of pacing is to help you build up how long you can do an activity for, so if that isn’t something that’s possible with your type of pain, it’s okay to skip this section.

Pacing isn’t about going to the gym or doing anything vigorous – it can be as simple as washing the dishes.

We’ve already talked a bit about good and bad days earlier in this guide. Pacing is about breaking the pattern of being active on good days and stopping on bad days – instead, you can stay at around the same level of activity all the time.

Pacing means you start doing an activity and stop when it gets painful, and then work on building up how long you can do the activity the next time you try it. Over time, you should be able to do the activity for longer.

It should be possible to pace almost any activity, even if it’s hard or feels strange at first.

This video from Pain Association Scotland has information on pacing.

If there’s an activity that you find difficult to pace, try our problem solving guide. It can help you consider new ways of doing things and fitting positive changes into your life.

How to pace an activity:

Choose an activity

Pick something that you find difficult, but not impossible.

If you can, make it an activity you enjoy that also makes you feel like you’ve achieved something, like going out for a walk.

Work out how long you can do the activity for without pain flaring up

This can take some time to get right, so don’t worry if it’s difficult at first.

At this point, only focus on how long you can do the activity for – so for example, if you want to go for a walk, don’t worry about the distance. Focus on time only.

Practice the activity regularly

Try to do the activity every day.

Try to practice on both good days and bad days.

Gradually build up the amount of time

Decide on a slightly longer time.

Start doing the activity for this longer time.

Keep building up the time until you’re comfortable and can do as much of the activity as you’re happy with.

7. Spacing activities

Spacing an activity means breaking it down into manageable parts, and taking a break between doing each one. By dividing up tasks like this, you can keep an eye on how you feel and how the task is going.

How to space an activity:

Choose an activity

Pick something that you find difficult to do at the moment.

For spacing, it could be a good idea to pick an activity you feel you have to do, like housework or cooking.

Break the activity down into parts

Try to think about each step involved in the activity you want to do.

For example, Mandy wanted to cook a meal from scratch. She broke the activity into the following steps:

  1. Getting the ingredients together and laying them out on the kitchen counter
  2. Getting all the tools she needs – pots, pans, knives, and the chopping board
  3. Chopping the vegetables
  4. Seasoning the meat

Mandy listed everything that she wanted to do to cook the meal.

Think about when you can take breaks

There are going to be places in each activity where you can’t take a break – for example, Mandy wouldn’t be able to sit down for too long after putting on the oven, or the food would burn.

Plan the places on your list when you can comfortably take a break without worrying about the next step.

Take the first step

Do the first thing on your list.

Even if you feel like you don’t need a break, rest between each activity on your list. The idea is get into the habit of spacing and get comfortable with it, so you can always finish an activity without pain getting significantly worse.

Section 8 of 20

8. Finding solutions

Chronic pain can limit your ability to achieve the things you need and want to do. This has an effect on your overall wellbeing, including your mental health. It can lead to disappointment, frustration, guilt, anger, and sadness.

Remember, there’s no shame in making your home work better for you and your pain. It’s not “lazy” or “wasteful” to make changes that make your life easier and your home more restful.

Finding ways to do the things you need to do can help you feel empowered, and more able to do activities you enjoy, even when your pain is bad.

9. Work and money

Chronic pain can often come with a financial cost. Dealing with things like cancelled events, missed appointments, and difficulty getting around can become expensive.

Pain can also affect you when it comes to work and study, meaning you have to take time off, ask for accommodations, or delay projects.

Financial help

If worrying about money is affecting your mental health, there are ways to get help.

Your condition may mean you qualify for the Adult Disability Payment (ADP), or other benefits.

Learn more about ADP

Learn more about other benefits for people living with illnesses and disabilities

Citizen’s Advice Scotland can help with other financial concerns

Learn more about coping with money worries

Work and study

As well as the costs associated with chronic pain, many people who live with pain on a long-term basis find it impacts their work and study. This increases your levels of stress and anxiety, and can also leave you feeling depressed.

If you need accommodations at work, there are options. You may be able to:

  • request flexible working
  • get help from your employer’s occupational health department
  • request a fit note from your doctor with information on changes that can help you work

If you are studying, you can ask for help from:

  • student support services
  • your student union

If an employer or educational institution is being unhelpful, you can get information from Citizen’s Advice Scotland.

10. Household tasks

Getting household tasks done when you’re dealing with chronic pain can be challenging. Sometimes it can feel like you have to choose between resting to cope with a flare-up, or getting things done at home.

Everyone’s pain is different, but there are a few ways to approach tasks that can help. Once you have a plan, you’ll feel more in control and be able to get more done.

Below you’ll find some ideas to help you work with your pain and complete your to-do list.

Peer support and advice

Find an in-person or online support group for your condition, or for people with chronic pain. Then you can ask people with lived experience for their tips and tricks, as well as sharing your own.

Find out about Pain Association Scotland self-management groups

Explore the Pain Concern online community

Focus on ease and convenience

If cooking and cleaning are difficult for you, explore easier and more convenient options. For example, you could try:

  • buying pre-prepared fruit and vegetables so you don’t have to cut and peel anything
  • using paper plates to reduce dishes
  • using laundry tabs instead of heavy liquid detergent bottles that require measuring
  • sitting on a stool in your kitchen to prepare meals instead of standing up
  • keeping specific laundry baskets, so you can sort clothes as you put them in rather than having to go through a laundry pile before washing

Think along these lines for your own home – what parts of tasks are the most difficult for you when you’re in pain? What are the options to fix them?

Share the load

If you live with other people, think about what tasks you find most manageable, even if you’re in pain. Then see if you can split your tasks so you’re in charge of those, and the others have the jobs that are difficult for you but straightforward for them.

You can also ask friends and family for help during flare-ups. There’s no shame in needing support.

If you’re struggling and there’s no one around who can help, your local Health and Social Care Partnership may be able to offer advice.

11. Thinking about pain

When dealing with chronic pain, a lot of people struggle with unhelpful thoughts and patterns of thinking. This can make it more difficult to manage their pain and develop healthy habits for dealing with it.

Patterns of unhelpful thinking

Knowing the common patterns that unhelpful thoughts follow can help you recognise and challenge them before they have a negative effect on the things you do.

Here are some common patterns of unhelpful thinking:

Emotional reasoning

This means treating emotions as if they’re facts.

For example:

  • “I feel like I’m lazy – I must be useless.”

Catastrophising

People often catastrophise when they’re worried about pain and its effect on their lives. They can feel like something that’s happened is far worse than it really is.

For example:

  • “I wasn’t able to meet my friend because I had a flare-up – they’re going to stop talking to me.”
  • “I had a good day today – I’m going to feel terrible tomorrow.”

Black and white thinking

People often see things as black or white when they’re struggling with pain – there’s no ‘in between’.

For example:

  • “I can’t cuddle my child – they think I don’t love them.”
  • “I can’t go to the gym – I’m so lazy.”

Must and should statements

People often have fixed rules for themselves about what they “should” do and how they “should” feel, and judge themselves harshly if they don’t meet these expectations.

For example:

  • “I must vacuum the whole house every day.”

When people think like this, they’re being critical of themselves, which brings their mood down.

If you find yourself thinking this way, it can help a lot to accept that things and people aren’t always perfect, and they don’t have to be for you to be happy.

Jumping to conclusions

People often assume they know what others are thinking, and the assumptions are usually negative.

For example:

  • “People at work think I’m taking advantage because I can’t do as much physical work as they can.”

Jumping to conclusions can also make you feel like you know the future, and that it will be bad.

For example:

  • “I lost my job – I’ll never find another one.”

Over-generalising

Based on one isolated incident, people with this thinking pattern assume all future events will follow a similar pattern. It becomes hard to see a negative event as a one-off.

For example:

  • “I wasn’t able to go on that night out – I’ll never be able to go out again.”

Dismissing the positives

Often people can ignore the positive aspects of life or situations, and instead focus on the negative.

For example:

  • “I played a game with my granddaughter, but I feel awful because I couldn’t take her to the park.”

Labelling

People who are feeling low often label themselves in negative ways.

For example:

  • “I’m a burden to my partner/family.”

12. Challenges to an unhelpful thought

Now you can challenge your unhelpful thoughts by asking these questions. Work through the questions below, using the examples to give you ideas.

Example:

“I wasn’t able to do any cleaning today and the house is a mess. My partner is going to be annoyed with me. I’m a burden.”

1. Is there any evidence against this thought?

  • “I’m usually able to do some housework.”
  • “My partner never gets annoyed with me for not doing housework.”
  • “My partner tells me I’m not a burden – they don’t love me for my cleaning skills.”

2. Is there any evidence for this thought (based in fact)?

  • “The house is a little dusty.”
  • “Sometimes my partner is in a bad mood after work.”

3. Can you identify any patterns of unhelpful thinking?

  • “I’m labelling myself in a negative way by saying I’m a burden.”
  • “I’m jumping to conclusions by predicting how my partner’s going to feel.”
  • “I’m using must and should statements. I’m setting fixed rules for myself about how much housework I should do.”

4. What would you say to a friend who had this thought in a similar situation?

  • “I’d say they’re being unfair to themselves – I’d remind them that when you’ve been in pain all day, it’s understandable you wouldn’t be able to clean everything.”

5. Is there another way of looking at this situation?

  • “This is a good opportunity to try pacing and spacing.”

6. Is there a proactive solution to this unhelpful thought?

  • “I’ll see if I can find a way to do some housework without making my pain worse. Even if I can’t do it, I’ll be proud of myself for trying.”

13. Goal setting

You may find you’ve had to give up going places or doing things that you used to enjoy because you’re afraid your pain will get worse. Or it might have become frightening for you to think about doing something new.

Setting goals for yourself is a bit like following directions on a map – you’ll find yourself reaching points on the way that show how far you’ve come.

Use goal setting to find the right activity level for yourself. It’s about getting some control back, and stopping the pain from taking over.

Here are some rules to follow to help goal setting work for you:

  • the goal should be something that matters to you.
  • your goal should be realistic – don’t pick anything really difficult
  • make sure you can measure the goal – so instead of saying, “I want to be thin,” you could say, “I want to lose 5 kilograms”
  • your goal shouldn’t be about making someone else happy – it should be something that benefits you and makes you feel good about yourself

Once you’ve decided on your goal, it can help to make an action plan.

If you’re not sure where to start, this problem-solving self-help guide offers helpful advice on breaking goals down and finding useful strategies.

14. Stress and rest

Stress can make pain worse, so it’s important to learn how to manage it. Pain itself can also make you feel stressed and anxious, creating a vicious cycle. Learning relaxation skills can help you to break this cycle, and manage both stress and pain in a way that works for you.

This video from Pain Association Scotland has more information on stress and chronic pain.

There are a number of breathing and relaxation exercises on NHS inform that can help with anxiety and stress. Try doing one of these when you’re feeling anxious.

If you’re struggling with stress, there is information on NHS inform about managing it and finding ways to cope.

Rest

Resting is an important part of managing chronic pain, but many people feel guilty about it, or don’t want to feel stuck in one place.

If you find it difficult to rest, remember that rest is the best thing you can do for your health in the longer term – taking it easy now likely means you can do more later.

You can also look at ways to make rest feel less boring. For example, you could try:

  • reading a book
  • watching a movie
  • listening to music or a podcast
  • learning a hobby you can do without much moving around, like knitting
  • catching up with a friend

Get more advice on resting and relaxing from Live Well With Pain

15. Assertiveness and communication

Chronic pain can cause you to lose your confidence, and make it hard for you to express your needs. If you can’t say what you need, you may find yourself dealing with more pain and discomfort, and feeling more tense – and tension can increase your pain.

Follow these tips to help you become more assertive and confident about communicating.

Say what you mean clearly and don't be afraid to be firm.

Don’t say:

“I’m really sorry but I think I need to stop, maybe sit down for a minute, if that’s okay?”

Say:

“I need to take a break – I can’t walk any further just now.”

Don't shout or raise your voice.

Don’t say:

“I need to take a break right now!”

Say:

“I need to take a break now.”

Remember you don't have to apologise for needing something.

Don’t say:

“I’m really sorry but I think I need to sit down for a minute.”

Say:

“I need to sit down for a minute.”

Make sure your message is clear – don't expect people to guess what you mean or know what you're thinking.

Don’t say:

“My back is a bit sore.”

Say:

“My back is getting sore, I need to rest for a few minutes.”

Explain to people why you're asking them to do something.

Don’t say:

“We need to stop.”

Say:

“I need to rest for a few minutes because my back is getting sore, so we need to stop.”

Ask for help when you need it.

Don’t say:

Nothing.

Say:

“Could you please carry this bag for me for a while? My back is starting to hurt.”

It’s okay to say no.

Don’t say:

“Yes, that’s fine, I think I can manage that.”

Say:

“No, I won’t be able to do that.”

16. Advocating for yourself

Many people with chronic pain find it difficult to advocate for their needs when talking to health professionals. It can also be difficult to communicate with employers, schools and colleges, or anyone else in a situation where you need accommodation.

As well as the advice in section 15, you can use the tips below to help you plan for conversations and appointments that could be challenging.

Prepare beforehand

It can be helpful to make a list of the points you wish to discuss, and the questions you would like to be answered.

Think about your goals for the appointment – what do you want to happen?

You can also try rehearsing the conversation, either alone or with someone else.

Be specific

If you have particular accommodation needs, or a course of treatment you’re interested in, be specific and explain your reasoning.

BRAN questions

Your care team should explain the benefits and risks of treatment options available to you. They should also discuss the option of doing nothing, and the impact that could have on your pain and long-term health.

You can ask health and care professionals the “BRAN” questions when discussing treatments and next steps:

“What are the Benefits?”

“What are the Risks?”

“What are the Alternatives?”

“What would happen if I did Nothing?”

You should be given enough information and time to make up your mind about every step of your care. Asking the BRAN questions can help you make an informed choice.

Think about the other person’s goals

If you are meeting with your manager, for example, think about their goals for the meeting. Do they want you to be able to get more done at work?

Consider what they want to achieve and approach the meeting as a chance for you to work together.

17. Sleep

Chronic pain can cause a lot of problems with sleep. You might find it hard to get to sleep, or wake up during the night because of pain.

Unfortunately, the more you try to force yourself to sleep the harder it can become.

Lack of sleep and struggling to sleep can also increase your stress levels, making the pain worse.

Read our self-help guide to sleeping better to help you deal with these issues

Section 18 of 20

18. Physical activities

Chronic pain can make it seem like exercise would be a bad idea – you might be afraid of making the pain worse. In fact, it can improve your mental and physical health to exercise and keep active, as long as you stay within realistic limits.

The most helpful way to approach exercise is to try to move in a way that feels good for you.

Avoiding certain movements and activities can actually make pain worse, because your muscles and joints can become stiff. This is called the pain cycle.

Muscles that you don’t use actually feel more pain than ones that are toned, flexible, and strong. Once you’ve found an exercise plan that works for you, it’ll be possible to be more active and feel better.

19. Coping with flare-ups

You might sometimes experience periods of increased pain, often called flare-ups. Flare-ups can happen quickly and without warning, so they can be difficult to cope with, but there are ways to make them easier.

This video from Pain Association Scotland has some advice on coping with flare-ups.

The amount of time flare-ups last is different for everyone.

When you have a flare-up it can be tempting to go back to the old habits discussed earlier in the guide, like avoiding activity. This can have a negative effect on your mental health.

It’s important to accept that flare-ups happen, they aren’t your fault, and it’s possible to cope with them. Dealing with flare-ups is a skill that you can develop and build on with time.

These tips will help you cope with flare-ups.

Think about the cause

Flare-ups can happen for no reason, but you may feel more in control if you can identify a cause. Did you do anything unusual before the flare-up happened? Have you been more active? Have you been feeling low or stressed?

Preparation

Prepare in advance. Keep a supply of any medication you use, and if there are items that help with your pain, like a heating pad for example, make sure they are available.

Stay calm

Don’t panic – tell yourself that it’s a flare-up, and it won’t last forever.

Managing unhelpful thoughts

Unhelpful thoughts like the ones covered in this guide can be especially bad during a flare-up. Make sure you use the techniques in this guide to help you think more positively – feeling angry or low can make you feel worse.

Medication

Take your medications regularly – don’t try to ‘be brave’ and avoid taking pain medications when you need them.

Adjusting physical activity

If you can’t keep going with physical activities during the flare-up, it’s okay to take a break. You can start doing them again slowly when you feel better.

Self-care

Be kind to yourself – use the techniques from this guide to help you balance rest and activity.

20. Next steps

Urgent help

This self-help guide is intended for people with mild-to-moderate mental health issues.

If you're feeling distressed, in a state of despair, suicidal or in need of emotional support you can phone NHS 24 on 111.

For an emergency ambulance phone 999.

While Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) doesn’t promise to reduce the pain, using the techniques in this guide can make you feel more in control, and help make sure the pain has less impact on your quality of life.

Keep using the techniques you found helpful from this guide – they should continue to benefit you. If there are some things that you didn’t find helpful to begin with, stick with them for a few weeks – CBT can take a little time to work.

Further help

If you’re feeling distressed, in a state of despair, suicidal or in need of emotional support you can phone NHS 24 on 111.
