1. Introduction

Urgent help

This self-help guide is intended for people with mild-to-moderate problems with anger.

If you're feeling distressed, in a state of despair, suicidal or in need of emotional support you can phone NHS 24 on 111.

For an emergency ambulance phone 999.

This guide aims to help you to:

  • recognise if you have problems with anger
  • understand anger and what keeps it going
  • find ways to manage anger and express your emotions in a healthy way

Problems with anger can be caused by a range of different things – this guide aims to help you understand where your anger is coming from, and learn ways of managing anger.

This guide won’t stop you getting angry, because anger is a normal emotion, but if you use the skills here it will help you to manage anger and express your feelings in a healthy way.

How to use the anger problems self-help guide

Working through this guide can take around an hour, but you should feel free to work at your own pace. Putting the advice from this guide into practice will take time. You can read the guide or sections of it as many times as you need to.

3. Underlying issues and problems with anger

As you’re learning in this guide, anger is a normal human emotion, but problems can arise with how we manage it. Sometimes problems with anger are a sign of other underlying issues that you might need help with. For example, it’s common for people dealing with anxiety and depression to experience anger.

People who have experienced trauma or loss can also have issues with managing anger. Traumatic experiences can leave you feeling a sense of injustice or sensitivity to threat. These are common triggers for anger.

Anger can also come from low mood, loss, and difficult life circumstances. If you think your problem with anger may be a sign of underlying issues like trauma, grief, anxiety, or low mood, you can discuss this with your GP.

For veterans who have worked in services and may have experienced trauma as a result, you can ask your GP about specialist services.

Section 4 of 23

4. Understanding anger

Anger is a normal emotion that everyone experiences. Anger can be helpful in some situations – it alerts you to a problem, and if it’s expressed in a constructive way anger can also help you resolve a problem. Anger can be a natural response to being threatened or attacked, and trigger our survival instincts. Getting angry can also motivate you to stand up for yourself, and express your opinion. However, if you aren’t able to properly express or manage anger, it can cause problems.

When people think about anger, they often think of aggressive behaviours like shouting, hitting, or breaking things. While some people who have problems with anger may lash out and do violent things, having problems with anger can also mean you don’t express it at all. Some people ‘bottle up’ their feelings which can lead to stress and unhappiness. Some people turn their anger on themselves in the form of self-harm because they don’t have another way to express it. Either of these responses to anger can cause problems.

Problems with anger can have a negative effect on your relationships, your work or your studies. You might find yourself doing or saying things when you’re angry that you regret or feel guilty about later on. These issues can affect your self-esteem and your mood. If you recognise these problems, following the information in this guide could help you to manage anger better.

What it’s like when you have problems with anger

Here are some examples of experiences you might have if you have problems with anger. There are others, and you might have different experiences.

Feelings you might have

  • irritated
  • annoyed
  • frustrated
  • stressed
  • raging
  • wanting revenge

How your body might feel

  • chest tightness
  • tense muscles
  • hot
  • sweating
  • light-headed
  • heart beating fast
  • shaky legs
  • quick, shallow breathing
  • restless – you might feel the urge to do something

Thoughts you might have

  • “This is so unfair.”
  • “They’ve disrespected me.”
  • “They’re deliberately winding me up.a”
  • “I can’t let them get away with treating me like this.”
  • “Idiot!” or other insults

Things you might do

  • ‘bottle up’ emotions
  • misuse alcohol and/or drugs
  • self-harm
  • react aggressively by throwing or breaking objects
  • ‘snap’, shout and swear, say hurtful things in arguments
  • storm out of situations
  • be violent towards animals or people

If problems with managing your anger are causing you to act violently, it’s important to seek help. You can phone your GP or explore local services to help you cope with what is underlying your problems with anger.

Section 5 of 23

5. What causes problems with anger?

There are many possible reasons someone could develop a problem with anger. It is likely that a combination of biological, psychological and social factors are involved.

Biological

  • signals in the brain get triggered (set off) in response to a situation or event
  • chemicals being released by the brain into the body (including natural chemicals, such as hormones)
  • survival instincts – fight/flight/freeze responses (there’s more information on this further along in the guide)
  • the effect of trauma, abuse or neglect on the brain and body

Psychological

A number of psychological factors can lead to problems with anger.

Personality traits

For example, some people are ‘laid back’, while others like to be in control and might feel angry if they aren’t.

Beliefs

How we view ourselves, others and the world. For example, “the world is dangerous, so you need to be ready to stand up for yourself.”

Values

What is important to you. For example, some people think fairness is very important, so they might feel angry when treated unfairly.

Thinking patterns

The way people think in different situations. You can read more about this in Section 9 of this guide.

The effects of trauma, abuse or neglect

Distressing experiences can have a long-term impact on your emotions and how you cope with stress. For some people, trauma can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and also affect your ability to manage your emotions.

Some people overcome the impact of trauma by using their own coping strategies and support network. Some people may need professional support to overcome the impact of trauma. If you have experienced trauma and it’s still having a negative impact on you months after the event, it might help to talk to your GP, who can tell you about services that can support you.

Find a GP on Scotland’s Service Directory

Memories

If a current experience reminds you of something negative in your past, it might trigger a reaction. For example, a memory of being attacked might lead you to react defensively.

Social

A number of social factors can lead to problems with anger. For example, inequality and injustice. Other factors include:

Environment

For example, if you live or lived in an area where there was a lot of violence.

Family relationships

The way your family handles anger can influence the way you handle anger.

If you’ve been taught to ‘bottle up’ angry feelings this can make it hard to express anger in a healthy way. If you’ve been taught to shout or be aggressive when angry or use violence to ‘stand up for yourself’, this can also make it likely you’ll have problems with anger.

Social media

If you have problems with anger, upsetting posts on social media can be triggering. Social media also makes it easy to make posts or send messages in the heat of the moment, creating more conflict in difficult situations.

Gender identity

The gender you’re assigned at birth can mean that from a young age, you’re told there’s a ‘right’ and ‘wrong’ way to express anger.

You might have heard phrases like “boys don’t cry” or “girls don’t shout”. This can make it hard to express anger in a healthy way, as it means going against how society expects you to behave.

Finances

A difficult financial situation can create a lot of stress, which makes it harder to manage anger.

Group influence

Other people can affect how you express and manage anger. If you feel pressured to behave a certain way in front of other people, it can make conflicts worse.

Section 6 of 23

6. What triggers anger?

Everyone has different triggers for anger. Here are some examples of times when it can be normal to feel angry:

  • being treated unfairly – for example, being blamed for something you didn’t do
  • someone doing something that goes against your values or beliefs – for example, insulting your religion
  • feeling threatened or attacked – for example, someone shouting at you
  • feeling criticised – for example, a partner complaining about something you do at home
  • other people not respecting you or your property – for example, someone breaking an item that belongs to you
  • feeling ‘put down’, insulted, laughed at, or embarrassed – for example, someone making fun of you in front of other people
  • being ‘let down’ by someone – for example, a friend breaking a promise
  • ‘losing out’ on something you wanted or valued – for example, not getting a job you applied for
  • being interrupted when you’re trying to achieve a goal – for example, someone distracting you when you’re in the middle of a task

When people are stressed, tired, or dealing with other problems, they’re more sensitive to triggers like these.

Anger can also be part of grief – experiencing a loss, like a death or the breakdown of a relationship, can trigger feelings of anger. If you’re struggling to cope with issues like this, you can find local services to help. Use Scotland’s Service Directory to search for wellbeing resources in your area.

Think about the times you’ve felt angry recently – do any of those anger triggers fit into the categories above?

If you find the same triggers come up repeatedly, consider if there’s a way you can avoid them, or manage the situation differently.

This guide can help you develop skills to cope with managing anger when those feelings are triggered.

7. Anger and the body

When anger is triggered, or people feel threatened, their bodies react in specific ways.

These physical changes happen due to signals sent by your brain because the ‘threat system’ has been triggered. This triggers the ‘fight, flight or freeze’ response where the brain is preparing the body to either defend itself against a threat (fight), run away from danger (flight), or stay still to reduce the risk of an attack (freeze).

It can help to understand what’s going on in your brain when these reactions happen in your body. Noticing the changes in the body can give you an ‘early warning system’ that you’re becoming angry, so you can take steps to feel calmer.

Anger and your brain

Frontal lobe

Your frontal lobe is responsible for planning, problem solving, and rational thinking. It’s the reason people can think about consequences before they act.

Amygdala

This is part of what’s called the limbic system. If your frontal lobe is your ‘thinking’ brain, your limbic system is your ‘emotional’ or ‘instinctive’ brain.

The amygdala continually takes in and processes information from your senses, so even when you aren’t thinking about it, it’s scanning for threats and interpreting what you see, hear, feel, touch, taste and smell.

When the threat response is triggered the frontal lobe temporarily ‘goes offline’ which stops you thinking rationally, and the limbic system takes over reactions and decision making. This is often referred to as ‘flipping your lid’. This is explained in the video below.

To reduce the threat response system and bring the thinking part of your brain back ‘online’, you can do things to calm yourself down. This includes using relaxation techniques such as controlled breathing.

Try some breathing and relaxation exercises

False alarms

The brain is hard wired to protect our survival, so it’s overly cautious. It responds in the same way to an imagined threat as it does to a real threat. For example, the threat response can be triggered by a horror film or a nightmare, when there’s no real danger.

When people have experienced traumatic events, their threat response system can become oversensitive and easily triggered. This makes false alarms more likely. This sensitivity can reduce over time by learning to notice false alarms and developing self-calming techniques and coping skills.

Section 8 of 23

8. How do problems with anger keep going?

There are a number of things that keep problems with anger going, and they often affect each other.

Avoidance

Anger can be an uncomfortable emotion, so you might try to avoid it. Some people might only have seen anger being expressed through aggression or violence. This can often result in people feeling afraid of anger and trying to ignore their feelings.

Avoiding anger stops you learning to manage your feelings or the problems underlying your anger. The more people try to avoid anger, the less capable they are of dealing with it and expressing it well, which leads to more anger.

Avoiding situations, places and people who might trigger anger also reduces your quality of life. Learning to manage these feelings can help you make things better.

Rewards

When your behaviour is rewarded in some way, it makes it more likely that you’ll repeat it the next time you’re in a similar situation.

Behaving aggressively can achieve short-term benefits. For example, losing your temper can lead to you getting your own way, or stop someone bothering you. However, in the long term, it causes problems.

If you tend to ‘bottle up’ anger rather than express it, you might avoid conflicts with people or be praised for being ‘easygoing’. However, this means the issues that are bothering you aren’t dealt with, which keeps your anger going and makes you unhappy.

The examples above are ways that anger can develop a repeated pattern, or ‘cycle’, of behaviour. You can also get caught in a cycle if you don’t have other ways of managing anger.

Coping skills

If you’ve never learned to manage and express anger in a healthy way, it can be hard to cope with feelings of anger. This guide suggests useful coping skills.

Section 13 has advice on coping with distress and Section 14 has tips on calming down and relaxing.

Problem-solving

Anger issues can be linked to a lack of problem solving skills. If you struggle to think calmly about problems, you’re likely to feel stressed and less able to cope.

Explore our problem-solving guide for more help with day-to-day issues

Communication

Often, people get angry because they can’t express their feelings, wants, and needs, or because they misunderstand what other people are trying to communicate. You might hold in angry feelings or have angry outbursts because you can’t express what you think or feel. Other people might be afraid to tell you what they think and feel, because they’re worried about how you will react when you get angry.

Assertive communication can help you express how you feel and tell people what you want and need.

There is advice on communication and assertiveness in Section 15 and Section 16 of this guide.

Rumination

Ruminating means thinking about the same thing over and over, without reaching a satisfactory solution or conclusion. For example, replaying a conversation over and over in your head, thinking about how it could have gone better.

It’s normal to go over what happened in the past in order to learn from it, but with rumination you get stuck. Some people ruminate over incidents that made them feel angry, and feel that they can’t stop their thoughts. Overthinking about negative incidents can keep you stuck in your feelings of anger, making it harder to manage your emotions and deal with problems.

Using mindfulness can help with rumination. There’s more information on mindfulness later in the guide.

Unhelpful thoughts

Negative automatic thoughts are unhelpful thoughts that pop into your mind, without you making any effort to think of them. They seem believable and are usually upsetting – this can affect your mood and what you do.

There is more information on unhelpful thoughts in the next section.

9. Patterns of unhelpful thinking

This section will help you recognise patterns of unhelpful thinking. Once you’ve identified unhelpful thoughts, you can start to challenge them. There’s a link to a thought diary at the end of this section.

Negative thoughts

Negative automatic thoughts are unhelpful thoughts that pop into your mind, without you making any effort to think of them. They seem believable and are usually upsetting – they can affect your mood and what you do.

Catastrophising

This means that you expect the worst to happen, and fear that you won’t be able to cope with it. You might think worrying prepares you for when something goes wrong, but in fact the time and energy spent worrying makes you more stressed. Then it’s harder to cope when things go wrong.

For example:

  • “If my partner leaves me, I’ll never meet anyone else, and I won’t be able to cope on my own.”
  • “If I tell my manager about this problem I’ll lose my job.”

Jumping to conclusions

You might find yourself thinking you know why someone did or said something to you – even if you have no evidence, or very little evidence, to base that idea on. If you jump quickly to a conclusion without having all the facts, then you might make a ‘snap judgement’ and react before there is time to resolve the misunderstanding. This can lead to anger and conflict.

For example:

  • “My partner’s not home yet, so they must be having an affair.”
  • “My doctor’s surgery called – they’re going to stop my medication.”

Mind reading

People often assume they know what others are thinking, and the assumptions are usually negative.

For example:

  • “She’s looking at me. She must be laughing at my outfit.”
  • “They haven’t responded to my text, they’ve obviously fallen out with me.”

Mind reading can often come from your own negative thoughts about yourself.

Focusing on the negatives

Often people ignore the positive aspects of their relationships, life, or situation. Instead, you might focus on the negative. This style of thinking stops you feeling good about yourself and others. When someone comes to visit you, you might think, “they’re always late,” and ignore the fact they’ve travelled to spend time with you.

Black and white thinking

With ‘black and white’ thinking, people see things as being at complete opposite ends of a scale – they don’t recognise any ‘in-between’.

For example, you might see everything as either ‘good’ or ‘bad’. If a friend cancels plans with you at the last minute, you might decide they’re selfish and a bad friend. You might think this way even if they’re generally a good friend to you and they have a genuine reason to cancel.

Another example is seeing things as either ‘right’ or ‘wrong’. If you have an argument, you might think you are right and the other person is wrong, even if you both have good points. You might think you have to ‘win’ the argument, and don’t want to ‘lose’ by compromising or accepting that there’s no right or wrong – just different views.

Must and should statements

People often have fixed rules for what they and others ‘must’ and ‘should’ do, and judge themselves and others harshly if these expectations aren’t met.

For example:

  • “I should always be right, or I’ll lose people’s respect.”
  • “I must be in control of everything.”

Must and should statements often result in feeling pressure to do things a certain way. This makes it more likely you’ll view a situation, and yourself, badly if things don’t go to plan. You may also place high expectations on others, which can lead to conflict in your relationships.

Sometimes replacing ‘should’ and ‘must’ with ‘I would like to’ or ‘it would be nice if’ can reduce anger.

Taking things personally

People who have problems with anger might be sensitive to criticism, and read too much into what others say or do.

For example, if someone doesn’t reply to a text right away, you might think they’re angry with you.

Another example is criticism of something you do, like a work task or a habit your doctor suggests you stop. You might see this feedback as a criticism of you as a person, and react angrily.

You can use a thought diary to identify and challenge unhelpful thoughts. Download a thought diary here, or go to Sections 21 and 22 of this guide.

10. How to challenge unhelpful thoughts

Once you’ve identified unhelpful thoughts, the next stage is to challenge them. To do this, you can ask yourself the series of questions below.

Can you identify any patterns of unhelpful thinking?

Look at the patterns of unhelpful thoughts listed earlier in the guide to help you with this. You can write them down if that helps, or use a thought diary which you can download here.

What is the evidence for and against this thought?

For example, you might think the way someone looks at you is evidence they’re thinking negatively about you.

The evidence against a thought like that is you can never really tell what another person is thinking unless they tell you.

Is this thought a fact or an opinion?

For this question, it’s important to determine if your thought is based in reality. Here are some tips for working this out.

If something is a fact:

  • there’s evidence that it’s true – for example, it can be proved to have definitely happened
  • it can’t be disproved because there is undeniable evidence that it is true – for example, if there’s a recording
  • it’s rational, meaning it comes from logic – for example, it was a real-life event and not just a strong feeling or belief

If something is an opinion:

  • it’s based on personal belief – for example, you might think your favourite sports team is the best one, but others may disagree
  • it can be argued against – for example, you can argue that dogs are cuter than cats, but others may disagree
  • you can change it – for example, your opinion of a person might change as you get to know them better
  • it’s often driven by emotion – for example, you might feel so strongly about an opinion that you get upset when someone else disagrees with it

What would you say to a friend who had this thought in a similar situation?

People are often kinder to their friends than they are to themselves. Think about the advice you’d give to a friend who was having a thought like this, and try to take that advice yourself.

Is there another way of looking at this situation that could be more helpful?

  1. Try to think about the situation as if you aren’t one of the people involved – how would it look to an outside observer? What would they think?
  2. Try to think of the situation from the point of view of the other people involved and consider what they might be thinking and feeling. Don’t assume you know what they’re thinking, just try to see the situation from their point of view. If possible, it could help to ask them what they think.

Ask yourself: why am I getting angry? What am I reacting to?

Often there are other issues and emotions affecting how you react in a situation. Think about what those could be, and how you might deal with them to reduce your anger. For example, are you getting angry at work because you’re stressed about a big bill you have to pay?

What would be a better way to deal with this situation?

Think about better, more constructive ways to manage this situation. There’s more information on coping strategies in the next section.

11. Coping strategies

People who have problems with anger often have a number of coping strategies – ways to feel less angry, or to try and let the negative feelings out. Many of these strategies feel helpful at the time, but can cause problems in the long run.

There are three main types of coping strategy: avoidant, emotional, and practical.

Avoidant

If you have an avoidant coping strategy, it means you try to avoid the problem, or how you feel.

For example:

  • drinking alcohol or using drugs to ‘block things out’
  • ‘storming out’ of difficult situations instead of talking it through
  • ignoring problems, for example not opening bills or not answering your phone

Avoidant coping strategies are short term, and don’t resolve the issue.

Emotional

If you use an emotional coping strategy, it means you try to manage the way the problem makes you feel, rather than the problem itself.

For example:

  • taking drugs or drinking
  • shouting at someone
  • crying
  • exercising or another physical activity, to ‘work off’ stress and anger

While these strategies might make you feel better in the short term, they don’t help to address the problem.

Practical

If you use a practical coping strategy, it means you try to deal with the problem.

For example:

  • talking through an issue you’re having with someone
  • using problem-solving techniques
  • seeking support or advice when you need it

A practical coping strategy allows you to work on solving problems and react to them in a constructive way. It can be difficult, but it has significant long-term benefits.

Avoidant and emotional coping strategies can be helpful in the short term, provided they do not involve doing something that could be harmful or illegal.

These short-term strategies can help when you don’t feel able to deal with a problem right away, so it makes sense to put it out of your mind until you feel ready to deal with it. However, it’s important to do something practical about the issue as soon as you can, or problems will continue to grow and create more stress.

Here are some specific strategies to cope with anger.

Reducing rumination

If you notice that you are ruminating on past events or things that make you angry, try the coping strategies listed below.

Coping strategies for rumination

Think about and try to notice what triggers rumination, so you can avoid it if possible, or use practical coping strategies to manage the triggers.

When you start to ruminate, try to direct your attention on to something else. Do something physical or that takes concentration, like a task or a puzzle.

Set aside certain times each day to think about what’s bothering you and if there is anything you can do to resolve it. If there is nothing you can do to resolve the situation, try to accept this, let go and move forward. Mindfulness can help with this.

Mindfulness

Mindfulness is a skill you can learn. It helps you direct your attention, so you don’t focus on unpleasant thoughts. It helps you focus on the here and now, which can help reduce the patterns of overthinking that can keep anger going.

You can build up mindfulness skills the same way you build up a muscle – with practice and exercise.

Learn mindfulness skills

Accepting anger

It’s not always helpful to try and stop yourself feeling angry. If you accept that you feel angry without getting caught up in the feeling or judgements, then the emotion can pass naturally.

If you sit with the uncomfortable feelings instead of trying to push them away, you can process your anger and other emotions. Remember acceptance isn’t ‘giving up’ – it’s a way of dealing with anger and moving forward.

It can sometimes be helpful to distract yourself or focus your thoughts on other things, but there are times when trying to ignore or avoid your feelings can make things worse. As you work on your coping skills you’ll get better at learning when to express your anger and when to focus on something else.

12. Managing emotions

Emotions aren’t good or bad. All emotions, whether they feel good or painful, are important.

Our emotions let others know how we feel. If we try to hide our emotions, then we might feel misunderstood and it can be hard to get what we need from people. It helps to learn to name and talk about emotions, so you can communicate what you need and how you feel.

Emotions make people want to take action, which can be a good thing. Emotions can motivate you to do something that helps you get past obstacles and challenges, and make positive changes.

Emotions let you know that something important is going on. If you’re getting angry about unimportant things, it could be a useful sign to you that something else is going on. For example, you might be stressed and in need of rest.

If you’re working on managing your emotions, it helps to learn to name them and notice what they’re trying to tell you. Naming your emotions is the first step to managing them. Keeping a diary can help with this. You can get one of your own, or there are links to diaries and worksheets in sections 21 and 22 of this guide as well as the one below.

Download a thoughts and emotions diary here

Emotion regulation skills

It’s normal for emotions to change, and go up and down, throughout the day or from one day to the next. If you learn to pay attention to the changes, it can help you regulate (manage and cope with) your emotions, even the difficult ones.

When you feel yourself getting angry or anxious, you can learn to pause and take a breath. When you feel yourself getting sad or lacking energy, you can do something to lift yourself back up – for example, listening to upbeat music.

When you take care of your overall wellbeing, it gets easier to manage difficult emotions. If you get a good amount of sleep, exercise regularly, and eat a healthy diet, it will be easier to manage anger.

You can learn more about taking care of yourself later on in the guide.

Managing unwarranted emotions

All emotions are valid, and there are no right or wrong emotions. However, some emotional reactions are unwarranted (not justified) because they’re too intense, and don’t match the facts of the situation. For example, if your partner goes out with their friends and you threaten to break up with them because you feel jealous, that’s an unwarranted emotion.

If you act on unwarranted emotions, it can have negative consequences. For example, if you shout at your partner for going out with friends because it makes you jealous, they could end your relationship. Sometimes unwarranted emotions stop people reaching their goals – for example you might miss a job opportunity because you are too anxious to attend the interview.

If you have an unwarranted emotion, it can help to do the opposite of what that emotion is telling you to do. For example, if you’re jealous because your partner says they are going out with friends, you might feel like shouting or storming off. Try doing the opposite, and say or do something nice for your partner or yourself.

Learning to notice when your emotions are unwarranted will help you manage how you react to emotions, including anger. Keeping an emotion diary can help with this.

13. Coping with distress

Learning how to tolerate (cope with) distress makes it easier to deal with challenges and difficult experiences. Distress tolerance methods allow you to bring difficult emotions like anger down enough that you can manage them using emotional regulation skills.

Acceptance

As discussed earlier in the guide, accepting difficult emotions like anger can help you to cope. Trying to deny, ignore, or avoid emotions can make you feel worse. Acceptance is not the same as giving up. It is facing how you feel so you can cope with it.

Distraction

Using distraction techniques can be a useful way to get past the worst of your distress and gently bring it down until the feelings are easier to cope with.

You’ll find some helpful distraction techniques below, or you could spend a few minutes thinking of some of your own.

Get active

Spend a bit of time exercising, or doing a hobby you enjoy. Even going for a quick walk can be enough to help ease feelings of distress.

Use your imagination

Picture something relaxing in your mind, like your favourite place. You could also imagine yourself coping well with a difficult situation.

Do something you enjoy

Use something you already have to make you feel happy. Watch a TV show or funny video, read, play a game, do some drawing or colouring, do one of your hobbies, or make a plan to do something nice later on.

Reach out to a friend

Phone a friend, send them a message, or write a letter or email. Building your relationships and getting involved with other people is helpful in the short and long term.

Create different emotions

Do something to create positive emotions instead of negative ones. For example, watch something funny or read some jokes.

Self-soothing

You can learn to calm your emotions and relieve distress by grounding yourself in the here and now, using one or more of your senses. Everyone is different, so practice when you’re calm so you know what will work when you need it.

Here are some ways to self-soothe:

  • sight – watch the changing colours of the sky, look at the clouds, go outside and take time to really notice what you see, or look at photos
  • sound – listen to soothing music, sing your favourite song, or go for a walk and listen to the sounds outside
  • touch – go for a shower or bath and really notice the way the water feels on your skin, rub lotion into your hands, or wrap yourself up in a soft blanket or jumper
  • smell – use your favourite body wash or spray, put on freshly washed clothes, walk past a coffee or cake shop, or go for a walk in nature and smell the outdoors
  • taste – have a comforting warm drink, slowly eat a square of chocolate, or eat your favourite meal

14. Calming down and relaxing

Doing some controlled breathing or taking a time out can help reduce your feelings of anger and calm the threat response in your brain.

Controlled breathing and relaxation

If you start to notice the signs that you’re getting angry, follow these steps:

  1. Start breathing slowly and deeply. Breathe in for 4 seconds, hold this breath for 1 second or more, and then breathe out for 4 seconds. Hold your out breath for 1 second or more before breathing in again. It can be helpful to count as you do this – for example, “in, 1, 2, 3, 4, pause, out, 1, 2, 3, 4, pause”.
  2. Breathe in through your nose, and out through your mouth.
  3. While breathing, try saying a calming statement in your mind during the break between in and out breaths. For example:
    • “Relax”
    • “Stay calm”
    • “Keep your cool”
  4. Repeat the calming statement while you breathe, until you feel calmer.

This exercise will help whether you tend to lose your temper, or if you hold your anger in. The goal of controlled breathing is to calm the fight, fight, or freeze response, so you have time to think before you react.

Time out

When your anger reaches a certain level, it can be important to leave a situation before it gets worse. The aim of a time out is getting away from whatever is triggering your anger.

During a time out, do something that calms you down – for example, do a relaxation exercise, listen to music, or go for a walk. This helps turn off your brain’s alarm system and gives you a chance to think.

When you take a ‘time out’, aim to walk out – not ‘storm out’. Let people know where you’re going. Avoid driving when you’re angry.

Once you’re feeling calmer, take some time to think about how you’re going to resolve the situation. Consider what triggered your anger to begin with and how you’re going to deal with the problem. You can also consider what you want to say to the other person involved, and how you’d manage the situation differently in the future.

Over time, if you keep using the methods in this guide, you’ll find that you need time outs less because you’ll be able to deal with situations before they get to this point. Consider time outs a short-term solution while you learn better techniques for managing anger.

Relaxation

If you’re more relaxed in general, it’ll be easier for you to notice the signs that you’re becoming angry. Feeling less stressed also reduces your sensitivity to anger triggers.

Try to find a small amount of time in your day to practice breathing and relaxation, and make it part of your daily routine. Do the exercises somewhere you won’t be interrupted. When you first start practicing these exercises, try to do them when you’re feeling fairly calm so you’re able to focus on and learn the techniques. If you’re already angry or upset, try using the controlled breathing exercise first, then try some of the other techniques mentioned in this guide.

It can take time and practice to feel the benefit of doing these exercises, so it’s important to do them regularly and stick with it. The differences can feel small at first, but as time goes on you’ll notice improvement.

If you feel any discomfort or pain when doing breathing or relaxation exercises then stop.

15. Communication and relationships

Good communication helps you and the people around you to understand each other better, and can improve relationships overall.

Often, people get angry because they can’t express their feelings, wants, and needs, or because they misunderstand when someone else is communicating with them. Learning to communicate well is an important part of dealing with an anger problem, because it can reduce a lot of the stress and possible conflicts in your life.

Communication styles

There’s a range of different communication styles, and most people use them all in different situations. There are 4 main types:

Passive

If your communication style is passive, you might:

  • put other people’s needs above your own
  • go along with what other people want, even if it’s not what you want
  • avoid expressing your own needs or opinions
  • find it difficult to say “no”
  • think of yourself as shy – you might tend to be quiet, and avoid drawing attention to yourself

If you have this communication style, you likely feel as if other people don’t listen to you. You might feel ‘walked over’, and often have to do things you don’t want to do.

Most people with a passive communication style are worried about how people will react if they express their actual thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs. You might be afraid that other people will get angry and fall out with you. This is an example of the catastrophising type of unhelpful thought.

Aggressive

If your communication style is aggressive, you might:

  • prioritise your own needs above others’
  • always think that you’re right, and force your point on others
  • always want things to be done your way
  • ignore what other people have to say
  • talk loudly or shout to be heard
  • talk over or interrupt people

This can be a confrontational style of communication. You might confuse this style of communication with being assertive, especially if it seems to get results when you want something. However, people might not enjoy being around you, be afraid of you, or see you as a bully. This can lead to problems in your relationships, and in day-to-day life.

Passive aggressive

If your communication style is passive aggressive, you might:

  • avoid directly expressing your feelings
  • be sarcastic or make jokes to show you’re unhappy about something
  • make rude comments, and pass them off as jokes if people react badly
  • try to make other people feel guilty for asking you to do something you don’t want to do, even if you’ve agreed to do it
  • sulk or give people the ‘silent treatment’ to show you’re unhappy

If you have a passive aggressive communication style, you might want other people to know how you feel without directly telling them. You might be afraid to tell people how you feel because you assume they will react badly.

However, by not communicating clearly, you’re expecting other people to read your mind – which doesn’t work. When people fail to pick up on what you are indirectly trying to communicate, it makes you feel more frustrated, and leads to more anger.

Passive aggressive communication can have a negative impact on your relationships and day-to-day life – people might struggle to know why you’re behaving the way you are. You’re also likely to experience frequent misunderstandings.

Assertive

If your communication style is assertive, you will:

  • balance your own needs against the needs of others
  • listen to other people’s points of view
  • say what you want calmly and politely
  • accept you can’t always have things your way
  • accept that other people have the right to say no
  • be willing to compromise and negotiate
  • behave in ways that show respect for yourself and others
  • forgive yourself and others for making mistakes
  • express your opinions and feelings in a clear and respectful way

Being assertive means expressing your own needs and wants in a way that’s calm, clear, and considerate. It allows you to express how you’re feeling and what you want in a positive way that’s more likely to be understood and respected by others.

Anger is often triggered by poor communication, so being assertive makes it less likely that you’ll struggle to deal with anger in your day-to-day life.

The next section has tips on how to be assertive, and allows you to practice positive ways of expressing your feelings.

16. Assertiveness and relationships

These tips can help you become more assertive and constructive when communicating:

Say what you mean clearly – don’t expect people to guess or know what you’re thinking.

Instead of:

Saying something sarcastic when you’re unhappy, for example, “that’s just great” if a friend cancels plans with you.

Try:

Expressing how you really feel – for example, “I’m disappointed you cancelled last minute. In future I’d like you to give me more notice if possible.”

Keep the volume of your voice steady and your tone calm – try not to shout or raise your voice.

Instead of:

“SHUT UP!”

Try:

“Would you please keep the noise down?”

Say what you think or feel using ‘I’ statements. Don't blame or attack the other person.

Instead of:

“You’re annoying me.”

Try:

“I’m getting annoyed.”

Listen to the other person and check you’ve understood their meaning. This means there are fewer misunderstandings.

Instead of:

Assuming or pretending you understood their meaning.

Try:

“Are you saying… [say what you understood]?”

or

“I’m hearing… [say what you understood], is that right?”

Ask for what you want or need.

Instead of:

Hinting or saying nothing – for example, if you’re struggling to complete a task on your own, just hoping the other person will offer to help.

Try:

“Would you help me please?”

This also applies to emotional needs and wants. For example, if your partner has a habit you don’t like, try being open. You can tell them how you feel about it, using I statements, and ask them to stop. This is an assertive way to communicate.

Respect the other person.

Always communicate respectfully, and think about how you would want to be spoken to.

Try to:

  • avoid insults or personal comments
  • accept it if the other person says “no” or disagrees with you
  • avoid getting aggressive or threatening

Be firm if someone’s trying to pressure you into something you don’t want to do.

If you’re having a conversation where someone keeps asking you the same thing, repeat yourself in a firm but calm tone. This is sometimes called the ‘broken record’ technique – if someone keeps trying to pressure you, you keep repeating yourself. The aim is for them to give up when they realise they can’t persuade you.

Remember that you don’t need to apologise or make excuses for saying ‘no’.

Be open to criticism.

If someone criticises you, they’re not usually attacking you as a person.

Try to:

  • remember constructive feedback can be helpful
  • ask yourself if the criticism is true or not before responding
  • try not to get defensive or take it personally
  • if you don’t agree with the criticism, remember you don’t need to pretend you do

Choose the right time and place for a conversation that might be difficult.

Try to:

  • choose a private place where you won’t be interrupted
  • pick a time when neither of you will be distracted or busy
  • try to talk to the person one-on-one

If you’re going to have a difficult conversation, plan what you want to say beforehand. You could try:

  • writing a script
  • practising the conversation

17. Problem solving

If you’re dealing with problems in your life, you’re likely to feel more stressed overall. This means you’re more sensitive to anger triggers. Using a problem-solving approach can help you cope better with the problems you’re having, and reduce your levels of stress and anger.

18. Looking after yourself

Improving your overall quality of life can be helpful in managing anger. Healthy habits can lower your overall stress levels and improve your mood.

Exercise

Keeping fit and active can improve your physical health, mood and ability to cope with problems. Try to get at least some gentle exercise each week. You could try going for a walk or a run, playing sport or joining an exercise class.

Find free and low-cost options for exercise in your area

Activities

Finding an activity or hobby you enjoy will make you feel better overall.

Here are some suggestions:

  • read a book
  • watch your favourite TV show
  • go to the cinema
  • do something creative, like drawing or painting
  • try learning something new

You could do a web search for events happening in your area and see if there’s anything you’d be interested in. Remember you don’t have to do everything at once, and you can try a few things until you know what works best for you.

Healthy eating

A healthy, balanced diet can improve your general wellbeing and mood.

Visit our pages on healthy eating and nutrition for information and help with meal planning and weight management.

Sleep

The ideal amount of sleep, on average, is around 7 to 9 hours a night for adults. Being tired can make you more sensitive to anger triggers, and leave you feeling unwell and overall less able to cope. Try to develop a bedtime routine and stick to it – this will help you get into a positive sleep pattern, which will benefit your mood overall.

Get advice and help with sleep problems from the Sleep Charity

Visit the Sleep problems self-help guide

Relationships and social support

Good relationships and support from friends and family can help you cope better. It can be helpful to talk through difficulties with other people. You could discuss your problems and ways of coping – some people might have been through something similar and could offer you support and advice.

Socialising and doing activities with other people can also be fun and improve your mood.

Alcohol and drugs

Alcohol can impact your ability to cope and control negative behaviours, which can be unhelpful if you’re trying to manage problems with anger. It can also make you more likely to behave aggressively, or act without thinking.

Drinking a lot and often can also damage your health. We recommend that you drink no more than 14 units of alcohol a week.

Many drugs can have an impact on your mood and behaviour. Using illegal or non-prescribed medication can be damaging to your health and negatively affect your relationships, work, and finances.

If you’re worried about your or someone else’s alcohol or drug use, you can find help from these organisations:

We Are With You (formerly Addaction) offers free, confidential support with alcohol, drugs, and mental health issues

The Scottish Recovery Consortium (SRC) is an organisation dedicated to helping people recover from problems with alcohol and substance misuse

Scottish Families Affected by Alcohol and Drugs (SFAD) offers support to anyone concerned about someone else’s alcohol or drug use in Scotland

If you’re using alcohol or drugs to cope with issues in your life, there is help available. Your GP can advise on services in your area, and you can also find a range of services and sources of support on Scotland’s Service Directory.

Self-care

It’s important to take time out for ‘self-care’. This means focusing on yourself and your own happiness for a little while, and doing things that make you feel good. Self-care could mean treating yourself to an activity or food you like, or just setting aside time to relax. For example, you could go for a walk, take a hot bath, or just settle in and watch your favourite film.

Self-care isn’t the same as being self-indulgent or selfish. It isn’t lazy to need a break. It’s healthy to look after your own mental and physical needs.

Prompts for writing your script

  2. Tell the person how you feel, using ‘I’ statements.
